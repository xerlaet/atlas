# Marie Bailly Miracle
---

In 1902, 22-year-old Marie Bailly was dying of tubercular peritonitis (severe tuberculosis of the abdomen).

She was put on a train to the healing shrines of Lourdes, France, though doctors warned she would likely die in transit. On the train was Dr. Alexis Carrel, a brilliant, agnostic French physician and anatomist, specifically there to debunk miracle claims. He examined Bailly, noting that her abdomen was massively distended with fluid and hard tubercular masses. She was semi-comatose with a racing pulse of 150, and hours away from death.

At Lourdes, she was too weak to be submerged in the healing baths, so instead attendants poured pitchers of water over her swollen abdomen as Dr. Carrel stood directly beside her, taking notes. Carrel recorded that over the course of 20 to 30 minutes, the swelling completely vanished and her pulse dropped to a normal, steady rhythm. Bailly sat up, asked for food, and walked. She fully recovered, joined a convent as Sister Josephine, and lived a completely healthy life until her death in 1937.

## Skeptical Response
Some skeptics say Bailly was suffering from a hysterical physiological reaction, where intense psychological distress causes the abdomen muscles to push outward, creating severe distension. The emotional shock and freezing water would allow her nervous system to reset, relaxing the muscles and flattening the stomach.

Others say that she had a massive, non-fatal cyst or a fluid buildup caused by a severe but non-tubercular infection. The sudden shock of cold water caused the cyst to spontaneously rupture internally, draining the fluid into her digestive tract or bladder. The theist will respond that this should cause some sort of immediate response, but that didn't happen.

While it's possible that Dr. Carrel misdiagnosed her, it does seem unlikely. When Dr. Carrel returned and published his eyewitness account, he was brutally mocked and essentially blacklisted by the French medical community. He was forced to emigrate to the United States, where he eventually won the 1912 Nobel Prize. In 1942 he finally converted to Catholicism.
